METHOD & VALIDATION

How FireRelay turns two fire records into one

For 25 years, satellites have counted fire hotspots, places where a pixel is hotter than its surroundings. The record is split across two sensor families that cannot be compared directly. MODIS (Terra since 2000, Aqua since 2002) sees 1 km pixels. VIIRS (S-NPP since 2012, NOAA-20 since 2018, NOAA-21 since 2023) sees 375 m pixels and finds many more, smaller fires. MODIS is reaching the end of its life, and S-NPP stopped delivering data on 2 November 2026. A fire manager who stacks the two records sees a false jump in 2012. Anyone who uses VIIRS alone loses 11 years of history.

FireRelay calibrates every month since January 2001 to one unit: NOAA-20 VIIRS 375 m equivalent hotspots. It then builds a burning-activity calendar for any area on Earth from that one consistent record.

01WHY NOAA-20 UNITS

Why NOAA-20 units

The challenge allows a MODIS-equivalent or a VIIRS-equivalent record if the choice is justified. We chose NOAA-20 for three reasons:

  • The future is VIIRS. NOAA-20 and NOAA-21 carry the record forward, and FIRMS asked users to move to them when S-NPP ended. In NOAA-20 units every new month is an observation, so no model is needed. Only the frozen MODIS archive is modelled, once.
  • Operational agencies already count in it. For example, the ASEAN Specialised Meteorological Centre in Singapore reports regional hotspot counts from NOAA-20. Its monthly reviews compare only the last five years, because NOAA-20 is the only sensor it uses. FireRelay puts 2001 to 2011 into the same units, so those comparisons can reach back 25 years.
  • It keeps the extra detail. A MODIS-equivalent record would throw away VIIRS's sensitivity to small agricultural and peat fires for every future month.
02DATA AND IDENTICAL FILTERS

Data and identical filters

All counts come from NASA FIRMS. The yearly archives are the standard, science-quality product: MODIS Collection 6.1 from November 2000, S-NPP VIIRS 375 m from January 2012, and NOAA-20 VIIRS 375 m from April 2018, all through December 2025. Months in 2026 come from the FIRMS area API, using standard data where it exists and near-real-time data for the latest months.

Every sensor passes the same filters before counting. We keep detections of type 0 (presumed vegetation fire), which drops volcanoes, static industrial heat sources and offshore flares. We also keep only nominal and high confidence detections (MODIS 30% or more, VIIRS "n" or "h"). Detections are counted on a 0.25° grid by calendar month and on a 1° grid by week. Terra and Aqua are kept separate because they pass at different times of day (about 10:30 and 13:30 local time), and fires follow a daily cycle.

03THE CALIBRATION CHAIN

The calibration chain: a relay

Like runners in a relay, each satellite hands the record to the next one while both are flying. FireRelay uses three hand-overs, each fitted where both sensors overlap:

  1. NOAA-20 era (April 2018 onward): observed NOAA-20 counts, used as they are.
  2. S-NPP era (February 2012 to March 2018): H = rS(cell) × S-NPP. Here rS is the NOAA-20 to S-NPP ratio over their overlap, April 2018 to December 2025. Its global value is , so the two VIIRS instruments agree closely.
  3. MODIS era (January 2001 to January 2012): H = rS(cell) × average over available platforms of k_p(cell, calendar month) × MODIS_p, where p is Terra or Aqua. This copes with the Terra-only months of 2001 and early 2002, and it keeps working if either platform stops.

Each ratio k is a ratio of sums: S-NPP detections divided by MODIS detections over the calibration months. It is shrunk toward its parent in a chain: 0.25° cell, then 1° block for that calendar month, then 1° block for the year, then the 5° tile, then the globe. For a cell, k = (ΣVIIRS + λ·k_parent) / (ΣMODIS + λ), with λ = MODIS detections, chosen on validation data. A cell with plenty of fires gets its own ratio. A cell with only a few fires borrows strength from its neighbours instead of producing a wild number. The ratios differ by calendar month because the size of fires, and so how many MODIS misses, changes through the season.

The MODIS ratios are calibrated on February 2012 to December 2019, when both MODIS platforms were still holding their designed orbits. Their overpass times drift in the 2020s, so later years would teach the model the wrong relationship for 2001 to 2011.

04DOES IT WORK? TESTED ON YEARS THE MODEL NEVER SAW

Does it work? Tested on years the model never saw

Test A, stable orbits: ratios fitted on 2012 to 2016 predict S-NPP counts for 2017 to 2019 from MODIS alone. Test B, orbit drift: ratios fitted on 2012 to 2019 predict 2020 to 2025. Errors are measured on monthly totals for every unit with at least 10 observed detections, at three sizes: 0.25° cells, 1° blocks and 5° tiles.

For scale, the two VIIRS instruments observing the same fires about 50 minutes apart disagree by a median of at the 1° monthly scale. That is a rough floor for any harmonization. The MODIS-based estimate is about twice that floor, which is expected: MODIS sees 6 to 12 times fewer hotspots, so its counts are much noisier. The harmonized calendar picks the same peak burning month as VIIRS in of region-years.

These hold-out errors are also FireRelay's uncertainty model. Every MODIS-era month in the app has an 80% range taken from the error distribution for monthly totals of that size, shown as a hatched cell, as an 80% range in the narration line and tooltips, and in the lower80 and upper80 columns of the CSV.

Worldwide hotspots per year. The raw sensors (thin lines) differ by up to a factor of 12. The harmonized record (thick orange) continues smoothly through the 2012 and 2018 sensor changes.

—SPOT CHECK AGAINST FIRMS ITSELF

Spot check against FIRMS itself

As an end-to-end check of the pipeline, including the near-real-time months, we counted NOAA-20 detections (nominal and high confidence) for October 2026 in a box over Central Kalimantan (111° to 115.9° E, 3.6° S to 0.9° N) directly from the FIRMS API: 79,630. FireRelay reports 78,472 for the same box, 1.5% lower. That is the expected size of the static-source correction and the partial edge cells.

05CALENDAR DESIGN CHOICES

Calendar design choices

  • Monthly by default; weekly as a deliberate option. Monthly is the clearest step for spotting patterns. A weekly calendar is far busier: 53 weeks for each of 26 years. FireRelay makes it readable the way a GitHub contribution graph is: one thin row per year, colours relative to the selected area, and hatching for estimated weeks.
  • Play the record. A timeline under the map plays the record month by month. The map, the calendar and an italic narration line move together, and each relay handover is marked.
  • Compared with the place's own history. For every month, the "vs typical month" view shows how many times the 2001 to 2025 median it is. Ranks and record markers show how unusual it is. Status cards answer the questions an emergency manager asks first: how the last full month compares, how the year to date compares, and when the burning season usually is.
  • Honest about provenance. Estimated months are hatched, partial months are dashed, and every tooltip shows the raw Terra, Aqua, S-NPP and NOAA-20 counts behind the number.
  • Accessible. The fire ramp runs from dark to light, so it can be read by brightness alone, which works for colour-blind readers and in greyscale. Above-typical and below-typical months use warm and cool hues. A table view is available for screen readers, and the calendar can be navigated with the arrow keys.
  • Built to last. The site is static files only: about 50 MB of compressed tiles, with no server or database to go down. Links are shareable, and the CSV records units, sources and uncertainty.
06LIMITATIONS

Limitations

  • Hotspots are detections, not fires. Clouds and thick smoke hide fires, and smoke is common during haze episodes, so counts are a lower bound. Counts are not burned area or emissions.
  • The MODIS-era estimate assumes that the 2012 to 2019 relationship between the sensors held in 2001 to 2011. Changes in fire size or land use can break that, which the uncertainty band only partly captures.
  • Areas are built from whole 0.25° cells (about 28 km), weighted by how much of each lies inside the area. Very small areas are noisy.
  • The latest months use near-real-time data, which FIRMS later replaces with standard data. Values may change slightly.
  • The tool is for seasonal and historical context, not for tactical, same-day fire response. Use FIRMS directly for that.
  • NOAA-20 is the single observed reference from April 2018. If it fails, NOAA-21 (in the same orbit since 2023) would need the same overlap cross-calibration that links S-NPP to NOAA-20. This is not implemented in this version.

Use of SI (AI) tools

  • Research: challenge and judging research.
  • Code: writing and debugging the pipeline and web app.
  • Writing: drafting this documentation.
  • Video: composing the demo video, which is watermarked as SI-assisted.

The numbers do not come from a model. Every number in the app is computed by the deterministic code in pipeline/ from NASA FIRMS data, and the validation numbers come from held-out data. No generated imagery is used. All visuals are charts and maps drawn from the data. The FireRelay logo is a simple hand-specified vector shape.
